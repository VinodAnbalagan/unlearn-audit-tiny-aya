Part 1 · The idea

A model is a library of facts

A language model like Tiny Aya, a 3.35-billion-parameter model from Cohere Labs that speaks over 70 languages, answers questions by passing your words through a stack of layers.

Facts are not stored in a database. They are spread across billions of numbers, the weights. Somewhere in that stack, "Which instrument does this person play?" becomes "guitar".

Where in the stack? A useful picture comes from research showing that the MLP inside each layer behaves like a key-value memory. Its first half acts as keys that recognise patterns in the input ("this is a question about Kotasel Virasal's instrument"). Its second half holds values that push the prediction towards particular words ("guitar")[1]. Later work located and edited individual facts this way, mostly in early-to-middle layers[2]. If facts live in these matrices, they are also where you would try to remove them.

Why forget?

Sometimes a model knows too much

Once a model has learned something, there are good reasons to want it gone. Retraining a large model from scratch costs enormous amounts of compute, so researchers developed machine unlearning: surgery on a model that has already been trained.

The worry
A model can stop saying a fact without the fact being gone. If the knowledge is only hidden, someone can coax it back out. That is the question this project asks: after unlearning, where does the knowledge actually go?
Part 2 · The test

Ninety-six people who never existed

To check whether a model forgot something, you must know exactly what it knew. Real facts make that impossible: the model met "Paris is the capital of France" thousands of times, in dozens of languages.

So, following TOFU, the benchmark that first tested unlearning on invented authors[3], we invented people. Each one has one fact: a favourite colour, a profession, an instrument or a pet. Every fact exists in English and Tamil, and a native Tamil speaker checked every Tamil sentence. TOFU uses 200 invented authors, in English only; ours is a small, bilingual version built to test one thing cleanly.

One twist makes the test fair. Each answer belongs to four different people, so the model cannot "forget" the word guitar. It can only forget who plays the guitar.

Two students, one difference

We fine-tuned Tiny Aya on these facts, showing it two phrasings of each question in both languages. It learned all of them: 1.00 in English and 0.99 in Tamil. Below are its real answers, including to a reworded question it never saw during training.

Then we trained a twin exactly the same way, except it never saw the 24 people we planned to remove. The twin is the gold standard: it shows what a model that genuinely does not know these facts looks like, inside and out.

Part 3 · The tools

Find it, then remove it

Two open-source tools from Lexsi Labs, chained together. CircuitKIT finds where in the model the facts live. SafeTune removes them from exactly those places.

Step 1 · CircuitKIT finds where the facts live
1 / 5Each of the 36 layers has a small network inside it, an MLP. Research suggests these MLPs work like the model's memory banks.
2 / 5CircuitKIT compares pairs of questions that differ only in the person. "Which instrument does Kotasel Virasal play?" gives guitar; the same question about Vasem Ramaval gives piano. CircuitKIT measures how much each MLP is responsible for turning one answer into the other. This is called[5] attribution (EAP-IG). We used 168 such pairs.
3 / 5Six layers stand out: 0, 2, 3, 6, 27 and 28. We ran it three times on different sets of pairs and got the same six every time. They sit at both ends of the model: layers 0 to 6, where it is still reading the question and the invented name, and layers 27 and 28, close to where "guitar" is written out.
4 / 5To test whether location matters, we fixed two more sets of six in advance: a "lookup" band (layers 5 to 10, the early-middle region where earlier research on other models located fact recall[2]) and six random layers.
5 / 5Only these six matrices, about 4% of the model, may change. Everything else is frozen. After unlearning on the circuit layers, our example becomes:
Step 2 · SafeTune removes the facts

Push one answer down, hold the rest steady

SafeTune's NPO unlearning[4] works like a tug of war. For the 24 facts to forget, it nudges the six chosen layers so that the right answer becomes less likely. For the other 72 facts, it pulls the other way so they stay correct.

We ran it three times, once per set of six layers, with exactly the same budget each time: 100 small steps.

Schematic, not data. The real training curves come in Part 4.
Step 3 · Looking inside

An X-ray of what the model is about to say

Checking the model's reply only tells us what it says. To see what it still knows, we used a[6] Jacobian lens. At each layer, it translates the model's internal state into the words the model is leaning towards.

If the right answer still shows up near the top of that list, the knowledge is still inside, even though the model does not say it. We compare that against the never-learned twin, so we know what "genuinely not knowing" looks like.

Step 4 · Checking our own work

How do we know the numbers are right?

An experiment is only as good as its checks. Four of them guard every number on this page.

1A judge that reads TamilAnswers are scored automatically, and the scorer accepts real Tamil spelling variants (Paris can be பாரிஸ் or பாரீஸ்) while rejecting near misses.
2Blind human auditA human relabelled every reply, without seeing the judge's verdict or which model produced it. They agreed on 142 of 144.
3Written down firstEach run's design, code fingerprints and predictions were recorded before it started. Two of our predictions turned out wrong, and we report them.
4Everything publicEvery run, including the failed ones, every model reply and every human label is downloadable.

Part 4 · What we found

Four surprises

Surprise 1

Forgotten in English, remembered in Tamil

The unlearning was trained and checked in English. Ask the same question in Tamil and nothing was forgotten. Each square is one of the 24 facts; coloured means the model still answers it correctly.

Layers edited
In plain words: the unlearning closed the English door to the fact. The fact itself, and the Tamil door, were left untouched.
Surprise 2

Unlearned is not never learned

Look inside with the lens. The never-learned twin still ranks the right answer in its top 10 for most of these facts. That isn't knowledge: "goat" is simply a sensible guess for a pet.

The unlearned models rank it far lower than a sensible guess. They did not go back to not knowing. They learned to avoid one particular word, and that avoidance is a fingerprint an auditor can detect.

Share of silenced facts with the right answer in the lens's top 10, near the output (layers 31 to 34). Grey: the never-learned twin on the same facts. Open circle: a decoy (another person's answer).
Lesson for researchers: always compare against a never-learned model. In our earlier pilot on real capital cities, the lens "found" 88 to 98% of silenced facts. Without a twin to compare against, that looked like hidden knowledge, but some of it may simply have been the model's sensible guesses.
Surprise 3

Replaced, never refused

A model that has truly forgotten might say "I don't know". This one never did. Every silenced fact got a confident wrong answer instead. Click a group to see the replies.

Surprise 4

The "right place" forgot the least

We expected unlearning to work best in the layers CircuitKIT pointed to. With the same budget, it worked worst there. Meanwhile the 72 facts that should stay never dropped below 1.00.

And two attempts to bring facts back

Nudging, and rounding

Steering: nudging the model's internal state
We built a "remember" direction from some forgotten facts and added it back on others. It recovered a few, but random directions of the same size recovered just as many. There is no special memory switch, only a generic "undo".
Rounding: saving the model at lower precision (real capitals pilot)
came back after rounding

Models are often saved in a compact number format called bf16. Gentle unlearning changes the weights so little that rounding erases the change: 22 of 24 facts came back. Drag right: stronger unlearning survives.
